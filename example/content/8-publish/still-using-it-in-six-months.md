# Will you still use it in six months? A test for any software deal

A good price makes a bad tool look useful. You have a folder of logins that proves it.

## Why do cheap tools end up unused?

Because the decision was about the price, not the job. Ben Ortiz says it out loud: "I buy
first and find the use case later if the deal is good enough." Sometimes that works. Mostly
it leaves you with a tool that has no place in your week.

Aisha Nelson, a freelance consultant, wants the opposite: "The only thing I want is to know
which of these I'll actually still use in 6 months."

## What is the six-month test?

Three questions. Answer them before you pay.

1. **Which job does it take off your plate this week?** Name the job. "Reporting" is not a
   job. "The Monday client report for 12 accounts" is.
2. **What does it replace?** A subscription, a spreadsheet, or an hour of your time. If it
   replaces nothing, it adds work.
3. **Does it work on day one?** Priya Shah's rule: "Needs to work on day one. I do not have
   time to learn another dashboard or sit through a webinar to get value."

Two yes answers out of three, and you will probably keep using it. One or none, and you are
buying the price.

## What does a tool that passes look like?

Sofia Marin is the only marketer at her company. Her job is one clean number every Monday:
"Monday is reporting hell. I'm in spreadsheets till noon and then someone asks why a number
changed." A reporting tool passes her test on all three questions. A second design tool
does not.

## What to do with the tools you already own

Run the same test on them. Keep what passes. For the rest, check whether a client could use
it inside a retainer. A tool you do not need can still be a line on an invoice.
